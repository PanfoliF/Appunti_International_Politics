
\section{Challenges to the Liberal Order: Summary}
\begin{center}
\textit{David A. Lake, Lisa L. Martin, and Thomas Risse (2021)}
\end{center}

\subsection{Introduction and Central Argument}

In ``Challenges to the Liberal Order: Reflections on International Organization,'' David A. Lake, Lisa L. Martin, and Thomas Risse assess the condition of the Liberal International Order (LIO) at a moment when it is under pressure from several directions at once. The authors emphasize that the postwar order has survived serious crises before, so its collapse should not be assumed. Yet the simultaneous interaction of internal political backlash, economic discontent, rising authoritarian powers, environmental and technological pressures, and attacks on multilateral institutions makes the contemporary challenge unusually broad. Their purpose is therefore both substantive and disciplinary: they ask what the LIO is, why it is being challenged, what may make it resilient, and what scholars of international relations failed to notice in earlier research.

The LIO has structured relations among the capitalist, democratic, and industrialized states since the late 1940s and has had wider global effects. It is associated with unprecedented cooperation among North America, Western Europe, and Japan, the emergence of a pluralistic security community, expansion of trade and capital mobility, high economic growth, the diffusion of democracy, and the development of an international human-rights regime. The authors do not treat these achievements as proof that the order has benefited everyone equally. Globalization created important distributional inequalities, and the LIO has performed poorly in areas such as climate change and the COVID-19 pandemic. Still, its record of cooperation and material improvement makes the intensity of current opposition an important puzzle.

\subsection{What Is the Liberal International Order?}

The authors argue that common labels such as ``American-led,'' ``open,'' or ``rule-based'' do not fully define the LIO. The order is dynamic, has changed across time and issue areas, and coexists with regional orders such as the European Union. They therefore unpack the three words in the concept: order, international, and liberal.

\subsection{Order}

An order consists of patterned or structured relations. Social orders may arise spontaneously, but they may also be intentionally organized through rules, norms, and decision-making procedures. The LIO is clearly rule-governed and sustained by coordinated action by both state and nonstate actors. Nevertheless, being rule-based is not sufficient to make an order liberal: the Concert of Europe and the Westphalian order also relied on rules. Liberalism therefore requires additional substantive principles.

\subsection{International and the Westphalian Order}

The ``international'' component of the LIO has to be understood in relation to the Westphalian order. In the authors' usage, Westphalia is not literally a system created in 1648 but a later order centered on sovereign nation-states, sovereign equality, recognition by other states, and noninterference in domestic affairs. The LIO and the Westphalian order partly overlap and have coevolved since 1945. They share principles such as sovereign equality, peaceful settlement of disputes, self-determination, and nonintervention. At the same time, the LIO goes beyond Westphalia through principles such as human rights and forms of legitimate collective intrusion into domestic affairs.

Figure 1 in the article captures this partial overlap. The liberal side includes democratic politics and economies, movement of goods and capital, human equality, rule of law, multilateralism, delegated or pooled authority, and collective security. The Westphalian side emphasizes ultimate state authority over territory and population. The common core includes sovereign equality, peaceful dispute settlement, self-determination, and nonintervention. This overlap is central because many contemporary conflicts arise precisely where liberal rules begin to constrain or penetrate sovereign authority.

\subsection{The Liberal Components of the LIO}

\subsection{Political Liberalism}

At its philosophical core, liberalism rests on universal human equality, freedom, and individual and collective self-determination. Political liberalism therefore implies the rule of law, representative government, political participation, and protection of individual rights. These commitments create tension with authoritarian regimes and, when international law reaches deeply into domestic political orders, with Westphalian sovereignty itself. Institutions such as the International Criminal Court and doctrines such as the Responsibility to Protect illustrate how the LIO can authorize constraints on domestic sovereignty in the name of universal rights.

\subsection{Economic Liberalism and Embedded Liberalism}

Economic liberalism has appeared in different forms. A classical version favors market capitalism, free trade, international capital mobility, and equal treatment of foreign investment. In the contemporary period, critics often describe the expansion of this model as ``hyperglobalization.'' A second, historically important version is embedded liberalism. The postwar architects of the Bretton Woods system accepted the benefits of international openness while recognizing that markets could create severe domestic disruption. They therefore combined trade liberalization with domestic welfare states and, for decades, limits on capital-account mobility. Compensation for globalization's losers was understood primarily as a domestic responsibility.

The distinction is important because economic liberalism does not automatically contradict Westphalian sovereignty, but the tension grows when international rules prescribe domestic institutions or policies. Neoliberal pressures for deregulation, privatization, independent central banks, or welfare-state retrenchment can be perceived as external constraints on democratic sovereignty. Migration intensifies the contradiction. The LIO broadly liberalized goods, services, and capital but never generally established free movement of labor outside the EU. Receiving states therefore confront a clash among economic efficiency, liberal rights, political inclusion, and national boundaries.

\subsection{Liberal Internationalism}

The institutional dimension of the LIO is based on principled multilateralism. Multilateralism means more than coordination among several states: it means coordination according to generalized rules that apply across cases rather than ad hoc bargaining based solely on particular interests. The Most Favored Nation principle in the GATT/WTO and collective security in the UN Charter exemplify this logic. Liberal internationalism also includes peaceful dispute resolution, the ambition to provide global public goods, security communities such as NATO, and multilateral arms-control and nonproliferation regimes.

Here too, tensions with Westphalia increase when authority is pooled or delegated to supranational bodies. Majority voting, dispute-settlement mechanisms, and international courts can constrain individual states. The deeper and more authoritative such institutions become, the more likely they are to provoke resistance from actors who want to restore national control.

\subsection{Universalism and Selective Participation}

The LIO contains a paradox. Because liberalism begins from universal equality, the order is in principle open to all states. Yet states can join important parts of the LIO without being liberal democracies. China, for example, has participated deeply in multilateral economic institutions while rejecting political liberalism at home. Illiberal states may join because of material gains, reputation, legitimacy, or the costs of remaining outside nearly universal institutions. Liberal states, for their part, have often welcomed such members in the expectation that participation will socialize them into more liberal behavior.

This produces a heterogeneous order. Some liberal rules have near-universal acceptance, while others are embraced selectively. Even liberal states differ in how much intrusion they accept: the United States has resisted institutions such as the International Criminal Court and, under President Trump, withdrew from the Paris Agreement before rejoining under President Biden. Conversely, some nonliberal states participate in intrusive human-rights arrangements. The LIO is therefore best understood as an evolving order whose components vary in reach rather than as a single uniform system imposed everywhere by the United States.

\subsection{Challenges from Within the Liberal Core}

The most striking contemporary threats come not only from geopolitical rivals but also from the internal workings of liberalism itself. The authors identify several mechanisms through which the LIO generates opposition within its own core members.

First, economic liberalism creates winners and losers. Trade openness, technological change, global production chains, and the financial crisis of 2008--2010 have produced geographically concentrated losses and contributed to rising inequality. These effects can spread through local communities, affecting employment, welfare, identity, and political behavior. The result is a backlash against globalization that feeds nationalist and populist movements.

Second, political openness can be used against liberalism. Freedom of expression and the open architecture of the internet allow misinformation, foreign influence, and strategies of ``truth subversion'' to circulate widely. Autocratic governments can exploit these channels, while domestic populists can reject the Enlightenment conception of truth based on reason and evidence. Climate-change denial, conspiracy theories, and early denial of COVID-19 illustrate how liberal openness can create opportunities for actors hostile to liberal political norms.

Third, liberalism has often relied on partially illiberal institutional strategies. Free-trade policies and supranational governance were sometimes insulated from popular pressure in order to prevent protectionism and secure long-term cooperation. Over time, however, this technocratic insulation excluded some constituencies and created resentment. International institutions appeared less responsive to citizens, and opposition could be mobilized around demands to ``take back control'' or repatriate sovereignty.

Fourth, liberal universalism collides with national identity. The expansion of rights, multiculturalism, immigration, and transnational authority raises the political question of who belongs to the national community. Migration is particularly difficult because the LIO combines strong norms protecting refugees with persistent state control over borders and citizenship. These tensions have become central to contemporary electoral politics.

\subsection{Nationalist Populism and Political Realignment}

These pressures converge in movements combining nationalism, populism, and authoritarianism. Nationalism prioritizes the interests and identity of a particular state; populism pits a supposedly homogeneous ``people'' against elites and pluralist institutions; authoritarianism rejects liberal constraints such as independent courts, a free press, and fair electoral competition. The election of Donald Trump in 2016, Brexit, and the growth of authoritarian-populist parties across Europe exemplify the trend. Similar dynamics are visible outside the Western core in countries such as India, Brazil, and the Philippines.

The authors also note potential realignments in domestic political cleavages. In the United States, an urban--rural divide has become increasingly salient. In Europe, the traditional socioeconomic left--right divide is increasingly supplemented by a cleavage between liberal/cosmopolitan and nationalist/authoritarian positions. These changes matter because they make hostility to the LIO part of ordinary domestic electoral competition rather than an external or marginal phenomenon.

\subsection{External Challengers}

External pressures reinforce these internal tensions. Technological change has accelerated economic displacement beyond the capacity of existing welfare institutions to compensate. Climate change threatens the global environment while creating powerful distributional conflicts because firms and groups with carbon-intensive assets stand to lose from effective climate policy.

China is the most important state challenge. Its extraordinary economic growth was enabled in part by participation in the open international economy, yet China remains authoritarian and strongly defends sovereignty and noninterference. It accepts many parts of economic liberalism and multilateralism while rejecting democracy, a free press, and intrusive human-rights norms. Whether China is a status quo power, a selective reformer, or a future builder of a more state-centered order remains unresolved. The authors emphasize that Chinese behavior varies by issue and that Western policy choices can influence the direction of this evolution.

Russia, Turkey, Iran, Saudi Arabia, and other states challenge different dimensions of the LIO. Russia especially favors a stricter Westphalian order, has violated post--Cold War norms through the annexation of Crimea and interference in other states, and has used cyber operations to disrupt democratic politics. Transnational Islamist movements challenge the liberal commitment to universal equality in still another way. The COVID-19 pandemic, although not an intentional actor, also tested whether states would cooperate through institutions or retreat into nationalism and unilateral border closures.

\subsection{The Erosion of Multilateral Institutions}

Internal and external challenges converge most directly in attacks on multilateralism. Multilateral institutions have experienced crises before, including the collapse of the Bretton Woods monetary system and the US decision to invade Iraq without broad UN support. What appears different in the contemporary period is the breadth of skepticism and the willingness of major states to weaken institutions without a clear systemic necessity.

The WTO is a central example. Even before the recent crisis it struggled to negotiate large new trade agreements, but it still underpinned the world trading system through existing rules and its dispute-settlement mechanism. Under the Trump administration, the United States combined protectionist tariffs with a strategy of blocking appointments to the WTO Appellate Body, impairing enforcement. At the same time, the United States withdrew from or attacked several other multilateral commitments, including the Paris climate agreement, the Iran nuclear deal, the UN Human Rights Council, and arms-control arrangements. The Biden administration reversed some of these moves, but the episode demonstrated that US commitment to multilateralism cannot simply be assumed.

The refugee regime illustrates a broader erosion of multilateral commitments. The principle of nonrefoulement and the 1951 Refugee Convention created legal obligations toward asylum seekers, yet receiving states have increasingly adopted restrictive or deterrent policies. The COVID-19 pandemic intensified this tendency: many governments closed borders, pursued nationally oriented responses, and competed over research, medicines, and access to scarce medical supplies. International organizations such as the WHO and IMF responded, but often slowly, while the EU eventually mobilized a very large recovery package. The crisis therefore showed both the weakness and the continuing necessity of collective institutions.

\subsection{Sources of Resilience}

Despite the severity of the challenges, the authors identify four reasons why the LIO may persist. First, the order has produced substantial benefits: reduced conflict among core members, economic growth, lower global poverty, and extensive international cooperation. These benefits give many governments and social actors a reason to preserve the basic architecture even while renegotiating specific rules.

Second, the LIO has generated vested interests. Firms have built global investments and supply chains that depend on open trade and predictable international rules. Although these interests have not always mobilized effectively against protectionism, they still have strong material incentives to resist deep fragmentation of the global economy.

The same interdependence that creates vulnerability can also reinforce support for openness. The US--China trade conflict and pandemic disruptions demonstrated how deeply production has been disaggregated across borders. Companies may struggle to organize politically, but the sunk costs embodied in global value chains make a complete retreat from international economic integration expensive. The authors also note that some unilateral US moves produced substantial international isolation, as happened when the Trump administration left the Paris Agreement and moved to sever ties with the WHO.

Third, the LIO is institutionalized in treaties, regimes, and international organizations. Such institutions are ``sticky'': once created, they can outlast the circumstances that produced them and act as shock absorbers during crises. NATO's persistence after the Cold War is a prominent example of this institutional continuity.

Fourth, the order has accumulated legitimacy. After decades of operation, many rules of multilateral cooperation have acquired a sense of normality and rightfulness. Actors may follow them not only because they calculate immediate benefits but also because compliance is seen as appropriate. This normative support can generate countermobilization when leaders openly attack the order.

Legitimacy matters because it changes the political baseline. Practices that once required active justification can become taken for granted as normal components of international life. When governments violate them, domestic and international audiences may react not simply because material interests are harmed but because the violation appears inappropriate. This accumulated normative capital does not guarantee survival, but it raises the political cost of dismantling established cooperation.

Resilience, however, does not imply that every component of the LIO will survive unchanged. The authors' argument is more cautious: institutions, interests, and legitimacy create powerful forces of continuity, but those forces now confront challenges that are both more diverse and more mutually reinforcing than in many earlier crises.

\subsection{Four Analytic Blind Spots in IR Scholarship}

The final major contribution of the article is a reflection on why international-relations scholarship did not better anticipate the present crisis. The authors identify four blind spots that should shape future research.

\subsection{Orders Include by Excluding}

International orders operate like clubs. Membership provides benefits but also requires acceptance of rules created by existing insiders. Exclusion, stigma, and the threat of losing membership help enforce order, yet the same mechanisms can generate resentment. States that remain outside, or aspirants that believe the standards for full acceptance continually rise, may form oppositional coalitions. Thus, exclusion is not merely a defect at the margins of the LIO; it is partly constitutive of how the order functions and of why it produces challengers.

\subsection{Orders Are Not Neutral}

The LIO embodies particular material and normative interests. Its rules privilege some actors, values, and policy models over others. Much institutionalist research emphasized how institutions facilitate cooperation and improve welfare, while constructivist research often treated widely shared norms as morally progressive. Both traditions sometimes underplayed distributional consequences and the fact that rules are written by particular actors for particular purposes. Cooperation can be Pareto-improving while still distributing gains unequally, and norms can be legitimate to insiders while appearing biased or stigmatizing to outsiders. The globalization backlash and debates over ``Asian values'' reveal these neglected conflicts.

\subsection{Institutions Depend on Social Foundations}

Institutions are not autonomous objects floating above society. They survive because social groups invest political resources in creating and defending them. Institutional insulation may sometimes be necessary to protect long-term cooperation from short-term pressures, but excessive insulation can sever institutions from their social foundations. Trade and supranational governance illustrate this danger: institutions designed to secure liberal outcomes became less responsive to losers from openness, thereby helping generate the opposition that now threatens them. Robust institutions therefore require continuing political and normative support, not simply legal durability.

\subsection{Domestic Politics Can Challenge the Order Itself}

IR scholarship has long studied domestic variables, but usually to explain foreign-policy outcomes such as war, trade, or cooperation. It paid much less attention to the possibility that parties, protest movements, territorial cleavages, and mass political realignments inside core states could attack the international order itself. The contemporary experience of Brexit, populist movements, urban--rural polarization, and clashes among social movements shows the need to integrate comparative-politics research on parties, elections, and contentious politics more directly into theories of international order.

\subsection{The United States and the Future of the Order}

The article closes with an open question about American leadership. Earlier theories often treated US hegemony as central to the creation and maintenance of the postwar order, while later institutionalist work argued that international institutions could sustain cooperation even as American relative power declined. The authors complicate the debate by stressing that the LIO was never solely an American construction: Latin American states and other middle powers contributed significantly to the development of both Westphalian and liberal principles. This history suggests that declining US leadership does not automatically imply the collapse of the order.

Three broad futures remain possible. The LIO could survive because existing institutions and old and new stakeholders defend it. It could erode toward a more traditional Westphalian order centered on sovereignty and noninterference. Or it could be transformed into a hybrid order that preserves economic openness and principled multilateralism while altering commitments to democracy, human rights, and international legal authority. The authors do not choose among these outcomes. Their central conclusion is that the present crisis should be treated as an empirical and theoretical research agenda. Understanding the LIO requires attention not only to cooperation and institutions but also to exclusion, distribution, identity, domestic political conflict, and the social coalitions that sustain or undermine international order.
