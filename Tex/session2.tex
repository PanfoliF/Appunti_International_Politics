\section{Seminal contributions to the study of parliaments through text as data techniques}

\subparagraph*{Bipolar system}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dominated by \textbf{two major powers}.
    \item Alliances and commitments are clearer.
    \item Neorealists argue that it creates \textbf{less uncertainty} and therefore \textbf{more stability}.
\end{itemize}
Bipolarity is thought to alleviate two of the conditions we have discussed that are thought to increase the risk of war: uncertainty and commitment problems.
\subparagraph*{Multipolar system}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Contains \textbf{more than two major powers}.
    \item Alliances and third-party reactions are harder to predict.
    \item Traditionally associated with \textbf{greater uncertainty} and instability.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Historical Examples an justificaiton for Neorealism}

\paragraph*{World War I}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Multipolar Europe had several major powers.
    \item Alliances and commitments were uncertain.
    \item Britain’s and Italy’s behavior was not fully predictable.
    \item This uncertainty supposedly contributed to instability.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Cold War}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Two clear blocs:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{US/NATO}
        \item \textbf{USSR/Warsaw Pact}
    \end{itemize}
    \item Commitments were much clearer.
    \item Used as evidence that bipolar systems involve less uncertainty.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Problem with the Uncertainty Argument}

The fact that multipolarity creates more uncertainty does \textbf{not automatically mean} that it creates instability.

Uncertainty may:
\begin{itemize}
    \item make states more \textbf{cautious};
    \item make some states more \textbf{risk-acceptant};
    \item have different effects depending on decision makers.
\end{itemize}

Deutsch and Singer even argue that:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{multipolarity may be more stable precisely because it produces uncertainty.}
\end{quote}
\vspace*{-1em}
\[Therefore:\ Uncertainty \not\Rightarrow Instability\]

\subsubsection{Problem for Neorealist Assumptions}

The claim that bipolarity increases stability is \textbf{not logically derived from neorealism’s basic assumptions}.

If leaders react differently to uncertainty:
\begin{itemize}
    \item this conflicts with the \textbf{unitary rational actor assumption};
    \item structural conditions would no longer produce similar state behavior;
    \item polarity would not have a predictable effect on stability.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Bipolar Balance of Power}
\paragraph*{Implication:} Strict neorealist logic suggests that bipolar stability requires \textbf{near-perfect equality of power}.

In a multipolar system, stability depends on \textbf{blocking coalitions}.

A coalition controlling half the system’s resources can prevent another coalition from becoming dominant.

Perhaps the most common claim among neorealist theorists is that war occurs only when both sides believe that their chance of winning is greater than 50 percent. This is a partial statement of what has come to be known as the balance-of-power theory. It invokes the idea that war is the product of mutual optimism by rival states, meaning that they overestimate their chances of victory

If the neorealist hypothesis were correct, we should observe no wars initiated by the weaker side. The evidence fails to support the hypothesis.

\subsubsection{Essential vs. Inessential States}

\paragraph*{Essential State} A state is essential if it is needed to form at least one:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{blocking coalition}
    \item \textbf{winning coalition}
\end{itemize}

Other states have an incentive to protect it.

\paragraph*{Inessential state}
A state is inessential if coalitions can succeed without it.

Such a state becomes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item expendable;
    \item vulnerable to elimination.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Example of a Stable Multipolar System}
Even weak state E is essential to some blocking coalitions.

Therefore:
\begin{itemize}
    \item other states have incentives to protect E;
    \item all states remain strategically relevant;
    \item the system can be \textbf{stable}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Example of an Unstable Multipolar System}
Coalitions can win without E.

Therefore:
\begin{itemize}
    \item E is \textbf{inessential};
    \item E can be eliminated without threatening the security of the others;
    \item the system is \textbf{unstable}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Main Implication for Multipolarity}

Multipolar systems can be stable under \textbf{many different distributions of power}.

Exact equality is not always required.

By contrast, bipolar systems may require a much more precise balance.

\paragraph*{Result}
\begin{quote}
Neorealist assumptions may actually allow \textbf{more stable power distributions in multipolar systems than in bipolar systems}.
\end{quote}


\subsubsection{Historical Record of Essential and Inessential States}

Inessential states are states that are unable to turn even one losing alliance into a winning or blocking coalition.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Essential states never become inessential.
    \item Essential states are never eliminated from the international system.
    \item Inessential states never become essential states.
    \item Inessential states are always eliminated from the international system.
\end{enumerate}

Each of these propositions is historically false.


\subsubsection{Bipolarity and Stability: War}

A possible objection is that war is uncertain.

Even if one state is stronger, it may avoid attacking because victory is not guaranteed.

Let:

\[
p=\text{probability of victory}
\]

A attacks when:

\[
p>U_{status\ quo}
\]

assuming:

\[
U_{winning}=1,\qquad U_{losing}=0
\]

War depends not only on power, but also on:
\begin{itemize}
    \item expectations;
    \item risk;
    \item satisfaction with the status quo.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Why This Contradicts Neorealism:}

This model assumes states can make a: $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{trade-off between security and power}

But according to Kenneth Waltz: $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{security is the highest end.}

States seek power mainly as a \textbf{means to security}, not as an end in itself.

Allowing states to risk survival for possible gains in power therefore conflicts with core neorealist assumptions.


\subsubsection{Overall Conclusion}

The traditional claim: $Bipolarity \rightarrow Stability$ has several problems:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{uncertainty does not necessarily cause instability};
    \item leaders may react differently to uncertainty;
    \item strict neorealist logic can make bipolar systems stable only under very narrow power distributions;
    \item multipolar systems can remain stable through \textbf{blocking coalitions};
    \item introducing probabilistic risk-taking creates assumptions that contradict neorealism itself.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Central conclusion}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The hypothesis that bipolar systems are more stable than multipolar systems does not logically follow from neorealism’s own assumptions.}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Bipolarity and Stability}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item First, variation in the response to uncertainty violates the unitary rational actor assumption, which contends that important choices in international politics are driven by structural factors, not by considerations internal to the state.
    \item Second, if different decision makers respond to uncertainty in different ways, then there is no reason to expect any empirical relationship between bipolarity and stability at all.
    \item Third, if leaders differ in how they respond to uncertainty, then the third hypothesis (that states mimic one another) does not hold up.
    \item Starting from the neorealist assumptions, we can show that it is logically true that more distributions of power are stable in a multipolar world than in a bipolar world. 
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{History and Neorealist Empirical Claims}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The international system was multipolar in structure from 1648 until the defeat of Germany in 1945. Thus, between 1648 and 1945 there were many major powers with no one or two predominating. $\Rightarrow$ The bipolar system began in 1945 and lasted until about 1989.
    \item Using Jack Levy’s (1983) classification of great powers since 1492, we can determine whether the longevity of the bipolar system was comparatively long or short. $\Rightarrow$
    \item Another way to evaluate the stability of the international system is to examine the frequency of wars among the most influential states in the world
    \item The UN Security Council (UNSC), then, by institutionalizing a multipolar decision-making structure, has provided a counterweight to bipolarity. This too may be an explanation for the long peace.
    \item The average interval between general wars is thirty-four years; the longest interval is ninety-nine years, much longer than the so-called long peace. It appears, then, that the bipolar, long peace cannot be considered unusually long after all and that bipolarity is not a superior treatment for the disease of major power war than is multipolarity.
\end{enumerate}
