\section{Bueno de Mesquita 2013 Chapter 13 -- \textit{Can Terrorism Be Rational?}}

\subsection{Terrorism as strategic behavior}

The chapter asks whether terrorism can be understood as rational political behavior rather than simply as fanaticism or madness. \textbf{Terrorism is defined as violence intended to alter government policy that is directed against people who do not themselves possess the authority to change or implement that policy}. On this definition, attacks on civilians designed to create fear and pressure political leaders constitute terrorism, whereas \textbf{attacks directly against military forces or government installations do not necessarily do so}.

Terrorism can be rational because weak actors may lack the military capacity to defeat a government directly. By attacking civilians, terrorists attempt to transmit fear through society and induce citizens to pressure their leaders for policy concessions. Democracies can be particularly attractive targets because democratic leaders depend on broad public support, face legal constraints on repression, and are expected to respect due process and human rights. These features can create leverage for terrorists even while limiting the government's available responses.

The chapter's central theme is that terrorism is often a problem of \textit{perceptions}, incomplete information, and credible commitments. Government policies intended to deter terrorism can change the beliefs of potential terrorists in ways that produce the opposite effect. Similarly, apparently irrational violence may emerge from strategic calculations made under uncertainty about how the government will behave.

\subsection{True believers, reluctant terrorists, and complacent opponents}

The analysis distinguishes three types of government opponents.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{True believers} prefer terrorism to negotiated compromise. Their goals are effectively indivisible: they seek total victory and are willing to use violence regardless of whether the government is responsive. Negotiation is therefore unlikely to moderate their behavior.
    \item \textit{Complacent opponents} prefer negotiation even if they fear the government may exploit or repress them; they do not choose terrorism.
    \item The most analytically important group is the \textit{reluctant terrorists}. They prefer a genuine negotiated settlement to violence but prefer terrorism to being repressed after exposing themselves through an attempted negotiation.
\end{enumerate}

Governments are also divided into two types. A \textbf{responsive} government is willing to bargain in good faith with weak opponents, whereas a \textbf{repressive} government prefers to exploit its advantage and suppress them.

Reluctant terrorists do not know with certainty which type they face. Their decision therefore depends on beliefs about the probability that an offer to negotiate will be met by good-faith bargaining rather than repression.

The chapter illustrates the logic with expected utility. Suppose reluctant terrorists value successful negotiations at 1, terrorism at 0.40, and repression at 0. If they believe the government is responsive with probability 0.50 and repressive with probability 0.50, the expected value of negotiation is

\[
EU_{N}=(0.50)(1)+(0.50)(0)=0.50,
\]

which exceeds the value of terrorism, so they negotiate. If they instead believe there is a 0.65 probability of repression, the expected value of negotiation becomes

\[
EU_{N}=(0.35)(1)+(0.65)(0)=0.35,
\]

and terrorism becomes the rational choice.

\textbf{Pooling Equilibrium:}

The same individual or faction can therefore choose peace or violence depending on its beliefs about the government.

When both true believers and reluctant terrorists choose violence, their observable behavior is identical. In game-theoretic language this is a \textit{pooling equilibrium}. The government sees an attack but cannot infer whether the perpetrators are uncompromising extremists or strategically reluctant actors who would prefer credible negotiations.

\subsection{Why ``we do not negotiate with terrorists'' can backfire}

A government's public declaration that it will never negotiate is intended to deter violence and reassure domestic constituents. Yet it can also provide information about government type. If potential terrorists interpret the statement as evidence that the government is repressive, reluctant terrorists can become more willing to use violence.

The chapter uses Bayes' rule to show how beliefs change. Assume initially that a reluctant terrorist assigns a 0.5 probability to the government being repressive. Suppose a genuinely repressive government always declares that it will not negotiate, while a responsive government makes the same statement half of the time for deterrent or domestic political reasons. After observing the declaration, the posterior probability that the government is repressive becomes approximately 0.67. In the numerical example above, that is enough to reduce the expected value of negotiation below the value of terrorism.

A self-reinforcing cycle can then emerge. The government's hard-line declaration raises the probability of terrorist action by reluctant actors. The observed increase in attacks persuades the government that it is facing fanatical enemies, reinforcing the policy of refusing negotiations and increasing repression. This, in turn, further strengthens reluctant terrorists' belief that peaceful bargaining is dangerous. Both sides can begin with relatively moderate intentions and nevertheless move toward escalating violence because each misreads the other.

The policy implication is not that governments should automatically negotiate with every terrorist organization. Rather, \textbf{some flexibility can create a separating equilibrium}. If credible opportunities for negotiation exist, reluctant terrorists may choose peaceful bargaining while true believers continue to attack. Their different choices reveal information about type, allowing governments to concentrate coercive resources on actors who cannot be pacified while seeking political settlements with actors whose grievances are negotiable.

\subsection{Multiple factions and the credible-commitment problem}

The second half of the chapter introduces a more complex bargaining problem involving three actors: a government, a moderate terrorist faction, and a hard-line faction. The model is designed to explain why agreements with some terrorists can be possible even though a comprehensive settlement with all terrorist factions may be impossible.

The government can continue the violent status quo or offer policy concessions, denoted by $k$. Moderates and hard-liners can accept or reject the proposal. If every terrorist faction accepts, they disarm. Once they have disarmed, however, the government has a strong incentive to renege because complete victory is better for the government than implementing costly concessions. Since disarmed terrorists can no longer punish defection, the government's promise is not credible. Rational hard-liners anticipate this and therefore reject even an apparently generous settlement. This commitment problem helps explain why armed organizations are often reluctant to surrender all weapons before concessions are implemented.

Paradoxically, the existence of a hard-line faction can make a credible agreement with moderates possible. If moderates accept a settlement but hard-liners continue fighting, the government still needs assistance against the remaining extremists. Moderates possess valuable information, local networks, and resources that can improve counterterrorism. Let $p$ be the probability that the government defeats the hard-liners with moderate counterterrorism assistance and $r$ the probability without it, with $p>r$.

The moderates will not provide counterterrorism assistance if the government reneges on its promised concessions. But if the government implements the settlement, the moderates may rationally help defeat the hard-liners when the implemented concession is preferable to the policy outcome associated with hard-line victory. Their assistance then gives the government a self-interested reason to honor the deal. A credible bargain no longer depends on trust or altruism; it is sustained because both the government and moderates obtain continuing benefits from cooperation.

The government must choose the size of the concession carefully. It wants the smallest concession capable of inducing both moderate acceptance and useful counterterrorism assistance. The relevant logic is that the value of the improvement in the government's chance of defeating the hard-liners must exceed the cost of the concession. In the chapter's simplified model, implementation is attractive when the marginal gain from counterterrorism cooperation is sufficiently large relative to $k$. The moderates, by contrast, prefer a larger concession and can bargain more effectively when the information and resources they provide against the hard-liners are especially valuable.

This creates a striking implication: a government may be able to credibly compromise with moderate terrorists precisely because uncompromising extremists remain outside the agreement. The hard-liners function as a threat that gives the government a reason to maintain cooperation with the moderates.

\subsection{Why successful negotiations can increase violence}

A negotiated settlement with moderates does not necessarily produce an immediate decline in terrorism. The chapter explains how compromise can simultaneously strengthen the government's counterterrorism capacity and increase the resources available to surviving hard-liners.

Before an agreement, dissatisfied citizens distribute support among the government, moderates, and hard-liners according to their political preferences and tolerance for violence. When the government makes a deal with the moderate faction, some moderate supporters accept the concessions and shift toward the government or withdraw from violence. Other former supporters of the moderates consider the settlement insufficient. Once the moderate organization abandons terrorism, these more radical individuals redirect money, recruits, information, or other resources toward the remaining hard-line faction.

Thus, after a successful deal, the government acquires additional resources and counterterrorism intelligence, making a government victory more likely. At the same time, surviving extremists also receive resources that previously went to the moderate organization. If counterterrorism succeeds in eliminating the hard-liners, terrorism can end. If it fails, however, the remaining terrorist group may become more violent than before. A temporary or even sustained rise in terrorist attacks after negotiations is therefore not proof that compromise with moderates was irrational. The correct counterfactual is what would have happened without the agreement and without the new counterterrorism alliance.

The chapter cites cases in which moderate or former terrorist organizations assisted governments against harder-line splinter groups, including the conflict surrounding the Real IRA after the Omagh bombing, counterterrorism against the FLQ in Canada, and cooperation against more extreme Jewish underground organizations in Mandatory Palestine. These examples illustrate how bargaining can help governments separate factions, mobilize former adversaries against extremists, and potentially end campaigns that cannot be resolved through negotiation alone.

\subsection{Land for peace and the Israeli--Palestinian problem}

The chapter then applies the commitment logic to ``land for peace.'' Such agreements are sequential: Israel would surrender territory now in exchange for credible peace and counterterrorism later. Once land is transferred, recovering it is costly if the other side reneges. Palestinians face a parallel problem because normalizing relations or suppressing militant factions before receiving irreversible concessions leaves them exposed if Israel later fails to implement the promised settlement.

Even sincere parties can therefore remain trapped by uncertainty. Israel may doubt whether Palestinian leaders have the willingness and capacity to suppress hard-line organizations. Palestinian leaders may doubt whether Israel will complete the concessions once they have acted against militants. \textbf{The difficulty is not necessarily hostile preferences}; it can arise from an inability to credibly commit to future behavior.

An additional counterintuitive implication concerns Israeli intelligence. \textbf{Moderate Palestinian factions can make a credible deal valuable to Israel partly by supplying intelligence and counterterrorism assistance against hard-liners. But if Israeli intelligence services are already highly effective, the marginal improvement from Palestinian cooperation, $p-r$, may be small.} This reduces the maximum concession Israel is willing to offer in exchange.

The very strength of Israel's independent intelligence capability can therefore \textbf{make Palestinian counterterrorism cooperation less valuable and shrink the bargaining range for a peace agreement}.

\textbf{What improves Israel's unilateral security position can simultaneously reduce the incentives necessary to sustain negotiated cooperation.}

\subsection{Overall conclusion}

Terrorism is not adequately understood by assuming that all terrorists are irrational extremists. Some are true believers for whom bargaining is ineffective, but others may use terrorism because they believe peaceful political action will lead to repression. Perceptions about government type, signals, and credibility can therefore generate violence that neither side originally preferred.

Hard-line policies can have unintended consequences by turning potential negotiators toward terrorism. Limited willingness to bargain can instead help separate moderates from extremists. Once factions are separated, negotiated concessions can be exchanged for counterterrorism cooperation in a way that makes promises self-enforcing. Yet such deals are risky: governments can renege, moderates can fail to deliver, and surviving extremists may receive new resources and temporarily increase violence.

The chapter therefore presents terrorism as a strategic problem of beliefs and credible commitments. Breaking cycles of violence requires behavioral change and calculated risk, not simply tougher rhetoric. Successful policy depends on distinguishing which opponents can be accommodated, which must be defeated, and what incentives can make governments and moderate factions credibly honor the bargains they reach.

\section{Kydd and Walter --- \textit{The Strategies of Terrorism}}

\subsection{Central argument}

Kydd and Walter argue that terrorism is best understood as a strategic form of political violence rather than as irrational brutality. Terrorist organizations are typically too weak to impose their preferred political outcomes directly through conventional military force. They therefore use violence as a form of \textit{costly signaling}: attacks are designed to alter the beliefs and behavior of audiences whose choices matter for the terrorists' political objectives. Terrorism works when governments or populations respond in ways that advance the terrorists' goals.

The article addresses four questions: what goals terrorists pursue, what strategies they use, why these strategies sometimes succeed, and what target governments can do in response. The authors identify five recurring political goals and five principal strategies---\textit{attrition}, \textit{intimidation}, \textit{provocation}, \textit{spoiling}, and \textit{outbidding}. The core insight is that each strategy exploits a specific kind of uncertainty about power, resolve, or trustworthiness and targets either the enemy government or the terrorists' own population.

The article therefore shifts attention away from the personal psychology of individual attackers and toward the strategic interaction among terrorist organizations, governments, rival political groups, and civilian populations. Effective counterterrorism requires identifying which strategic logic is operating and choosing a response that does not reinforce it.

\subsection{Terrorist goals}

The authors define terrorism as the use of violence against civilians by nonstate actors for political purposes. The individual attacker may be motivated by religion, revenge, ideology, material rewards, or personal grievances, but terrorist organizations usually pursue broader political objectives. Their violence is therefore instrumental rather than simply expressive.

Five goals recur across terrorist organization:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Regime change} seeks to overthrow an existing government and replace it with one more favorable to the terrorist organization. Marxist revolutionary groups such as Peru's Shining Path and Islamist organizations seeking Islamic governments illustrate this objective.
    
    \item \textit{Territorial change} seeks either independence for a particular territory or its transfer to another state. Examples include the Tamil Tigers' campaign for an independent Tamil homeland and militant groups seeking to detach Kashmir from India.
    
    \item \textit{Policy change} involves narrower demands that do not require destroying the existing regime. Al-Qaida's demands that the United States reduce its military and political involvement in the Middle East illustrate this category.
    
    \item \textit{Social control} aims directly at civilian behavior rather than government policy. Terrorist organizations may use violence to compel communities to follow religious, racial, social, or moral rules, as when anti-abortion extremists attack doctors to discourage abortion provision.
    
    \item Finally, \textit{status quo maintenance} involves defending an existing political or territorial arrangement against groups seeking change. Right-wing paramilitaries and some loyalist organizations have used terrorism in this way.
\end{enumerate}

The State Department's list of foreign terrorist organizations discussed in the article shows that regime and territorial change are especially common. Many organizations pursue multiple objectives at the same time.

\textbf{Terrorist violence usually serves identifiable political purposes.}

\subsection{Terrorism as costly signaling}

The theoretical foundation of the article is the bargaining and signaling literature. Conflict often occurs because actors possess incomplete information about one another's power, resolve, or intentions. Ordinary verbal communication may not solve this problem because actors have incentives to bluff. Statements of determination or moderation are therefore often dismissed as \textit{cheap talk}.

Costly signals are different because they impose costs that actors with weaker preferences or fewer capabilities may be unwilling to bear. Terrorist violence plays a similar role for weak nonstate actors.

Terrorists principally seek to influence \textbf{two audiences}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The first is the \textit{enemy government}, which can make policy or territorial concessions.
    
    \item The second is the terrorists' \textit{own population}, whose support, obedience, money, recruits, and political allegiance are necessary for organizational survival.
\end{enumerate}
Combining these two audiences with uncertainty about power, resolve, and trustworthiness produces the five strategies analyzed in the article.

The article's central diagram organizes these strategies. When the enemy is uncertain about terrorist \textit{power or resolve}, terrorists can use \textit{attrition}. When the terrorists' own population is uncertain about terrorist \textit{power}, the group can use \textit{intimidation}; when it is uncertain about the terrorists' \textit{resolve}, the group can use \textit{outbidding}. When uncertainty concerns \textit{trustworthiness}, terrorists can use \textit{spoiling} to influence the enemy or \textit{provocation} to influence their own population. This framework makes the five strategies parts of a common theory rather than a loose list of tactics.

\subsection{Attrition: a battle of wills}

In an attrition strategy, terrorists attempt to persuade the enemy government that they are sufficiently strong and determined to impose continuing costs. The objective is to convince the target that maintaining the disputed policy is more costly than granting concessions. Terrorist attacks therefore function as evidence of the organization's capacity and willingness to continue fighting.

Attrition is especially relevant to territorial and policy disputes. The terrorist organization repeatedly imposes casualties or economic and psychological costs until the target recalculates the value of resistance. Kydd and Walter draw heavily on Pape's analysis of suicide terrorism, which interprets suicide attacks as a weapon used by weak actors that cannot win conventionally but can impose painful costs on a stronger adversary.

Three conditions shape the effectiveness of attrition:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The first is the target state's \textit{interest in the disputed issue}. Governments are more likely to concede when the issue is peripheral and less likely when vital interests are at stake. The United States withdrew from Lebanon after the 1983 Marine barracks bombing partly because Lebanon was not a vital interest. By contrast, the September 11 attacks did not induce the United States to abandon the Middle East because the interests involved were much larger.
    
    \item The second condition is the target's \textit{ability to retaliate}. Terrorist organizations are vulnerable because they are weaker than states. Attrition is more attractive when governments are constrained in their use of force or have difficulty locating the terrorist organization. Democratic governments may face stronger legal, political, and normative constraints on indiscriminate retaliation, while dispersed organizations such as al-Qaida can be difficult to target effectively.
    
    \item The third condition is the target's \textit{cost tolerance}. Terrorists prefer opponents they believe will be sensitive to casualties, economic disruption, or fear. Democracies may appear especially vulnerable because citizens can pressure elected governments and because a free press makes violence highly visible. Terrorists also learn from past concessions. A state that has previously withdrawn under pressure may acquire a reputation for low cost tolerance, encouraging future campaigns.
\end{enumerate}

The authors discuss several best responses to attrition. Governments may concede issues that are genuinely unimportant, while demonstrating firmness on more important issues to protect their reputation. If concessions are unacceptable, retaliation should be precise and targeted so that it does not simultaneously facilitate provocation. Governments should also harden vulnerable targets, deny terrorists access to especially destructive weapons, and reduce the psychological effects of terrorism by avoiding exaggerated public rhetoric and self-imposed costs. The key is to prevent terrorists from demonstrating that continued attacks can impose unbearable costs.

\subsection{Intimidation: the reign of terror}

\textbf{Intimidation is directed mainly at the terrorist organization's own population}. It is a strategy of deterrence: terrorists seek to convince civilians that disobedience will be punished and that the government is unable to protect them. The strategy is particularly useful for regime change and social control.

When terrorists seek regime change, they must weaken public support for the state. Selective attacks against mayors, police officers, prosecutors, election workers, informants, or other visible supporters of the government demonstrate the organization's ability to punish collaboration. Terrorists can also impose social rules directly by threatening civilians. Attacks on abortion clinics or schools for girls are examples in which violence is used to deter conduct the terrorists oppose.

Intimidation is most effective where the state is weak, its territory is difficult to control, and law-enforcement institutions cannot reliably punish perpetrators.

In civil wars, civilians caught between government and rebel forces may support whichever actor can credibly protect or punish them. Weak courts and police institutions similarly facilitate social-control strategies.

\textbf{The best response is therefore to increase the state's ability to provide security.} For territorial insurgencies, Kydd and Walter advocate a \textit{clear-and-hold} approach: the \textbf{government should establish unambiguous control over particular areas} rather than conducting temporary raids that leave civilians exposed to later retaliation. If the government cannot completely exclude terrorists, it should increase \textbf{protection for likely targets} such as officials, election workers, police, and community leaders.

Where intimidation seeks social control, stronger law enforcement, prosecution, and protection of threatened individuals are essential. The strategic goal is to destroy the terrorist organization's claim that it can punish civilians with impunity.

\subsection{Provocation: lighting the fuse}

\textbf{Provocation is designed to manipulate the terrorists' own population by inducing the enemy government to respond indiscriminately.} Terrorists want a reaction that harms civilians and therefore makes the target government appear brutal, hostile, and untrustworthy. The resulting anger and fear can radicalize previously moderate citizens and increase recruitment and support for the terrorist organization.

Provocation is especially useful for groups seeking regime or territorial change because they often begin with preferences more extreme than those of the broader population they claim to represent. To succeed, they need moderates to conclude that coexistence or compromise with the government is impossible. Government overreaction can provide exactly this information.

The strategy works best when the target is capable of substantial repression but not so unconstrained that it can destroy the terrorist constituency completely. A genocidal regime is a poor target because provocation may lead to annihilation rather than mobilization. At the opposite extreme, a government that responds only with highly discriminating law enforcement is difficult to provoke.

Domestic politics can make democratic governments vulnerable. Citizens demand visible action after attacks, but many of the most effective counterterrorism tools---intelligence work, infiltration, surveillance, and cooperation among agencies---are largely invisible. Elected leaders may therefore choose highly visible military responses even when these are strategically counterproductive. The authors interpret al-Qaida's strategy toward the United States in this light: the September 11 attacks were intended not only to impose costs but also to provoke a large U.S. military response that could increase anti-American sentiment and mobilize support for al-Qaida.

\textbf{The appropriate response to provocation is highly discriminating counterterrorism. Governments should isolate terrorists from the broader population, improve intelligence, avoid unnecessary civilian casualties, and demonstrate that their conflict is with the terrorist organization rather than the community from which it recruits.} Economic and social assistance to moderate communities can also counter the terrorist claim that the state is fundamentally hostile.

\subsection{Spoiling: sabotaging peace}

\textbf{Spoiling occurs when extremists use terrorism to undermine negotiations between moderates on their own side and the enemy government.} The strategy exploits uncertainty about whether the moderates are trustworthy and capable of enforcing a peace agreement. Terrorist attacks are designed to persuade the enemy that the moderate negotiating partner either secretly supports violence or lacks the ability to stop it.

The strategy is particularly attractive when peace negotiations threaten the terrorists' more extreme objectives. If a compromise becomes successful, the broader population may stop supporting continued violence, leaving extremist groups politically isolated. \textbf{Spoilers therefore attack at moments when negotiations, elections, or treaty implementation make mistrust especially consequential.}

The Israeli--Palestinian peace process provides the article's main example. \textbf{Hamas attacks often occurred around critical moments in the Oslo process.} Violence could encourage Israelis to infer that Palestinian moderates were unwilling or unable to restrain extremists, weakening support for further concessions. The strategy can also affect elections by shifting voters toward hard-line parties less willing to negotiate.

Spoiling is most likely to succeed when the target believes that moderate leaders are sufficiently strong to suppress terrorism. Paradoxically, strong moderates can be especially vulnerable to the spoiler problem: if attacks continue, the enemy may conclude that the moderates are tolerating them deliberately. When moderates are obviously weak, terrorist attacks reveal less about their intentions.

The best responses are mechanisms that increase trust and reduce vulnerability. \textbf{Third-party monitoring can verify whether each side is implementing its obligations}, while third-party enforcement can make violations more costly. \textbf{Peace agreements can also be designed} to reduce dangerous shifts in relative power, preserve some defensive capabilities, institutionalize minority protections, or embed the settlement in international organizations that increase the costs of exploitation. \textbf{These measures address the commitment problems on which spoilers rely.}

\subsection{Outbidding: zealots versus sellouts}

Outbidding is a competition among organizations seeking support from the same domestic constituency. It occurs when multiple groups compete for leadership and citizens are uncertain about which organization is most committed to defending their interests. \textbf{Terrorist violence becomes a signal of resolve}: by carrying out costly attacks, a group demonstrates that it is a genuine ``zealot'' rather than a weak ``sellout.''

{The strategy does not primarily target the enemy government. Its principal audience is the terrorists' own population.} Citizens may prefer leaders who appear somewhat more hard-line than themselves because such leaders can bargain more effectively, resist an untrustworthy enemy, or avoid the moderating temptations of office. Groups therefore have incentives to prove their militancy by escalating violence.

Outbidding is most likely when several organizations compete for the allegiance of the same social or political base. The rivalry between Hamas and Fatah illustrates this logic, as does competition among radical leftist movements in Peru. \textbf{Violence may continue even when it appears to produce few concessions from the enemy because the real purpose is to win a domestic political contest.}

One response is to reduce competition by encouraging rival factions to consolidate into a unified opposition. This can reduce incentives for groups to demonstrate superior militancy, although a unified opposition may also become politically stronger. \textbf{A second response is to make nonviolent political participation visibly successful. Governments can grant meaningful concessions to peaceful actors}, provide services and resources to their constituencies, and protect moderates from violent rivals. If peaceful strategies yield better results than terrorism, outbidding becomes less attractive.

\subsection{Information, regime type, and counterterrorism}

The article concludes that information is the common thread connecting the five strategies. Terrorist violence matters because audiences are uncertain. Attrition communicates information about power and resolve; intimidation communicates the ability to punish; provocation manipulates beliefs about the enemy's intentions; spoiling manipulates beliefs about moderates' trustworthiness; and outbidding manipulates beliefs about rival groups' commitment.

Counterterrorism should therefore be understood less as a generic ``war'' and more as a struggle over information, beliefs, and strategic incentives. \textbf{Intelligence is essential not merely because it helps states find terrorists but because it allows governments to distinguish among strategies and respond precisely.} A government that misunderstands the terrorist strategy can unintentionally strengthen it. For example, indiscriminate retaliation against an attrition campaign may increase recruitment through provocation, while search-and-destroy operations against an intimidation campaign may fail to protect civilians and therefore reinforce terrorist control.

Regime type also matters:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Democracies appear particularly vulnerable to attrition and provocation because they are sensitive to public costs, constrained in their use of indiscriminate force, and under pressure to respond visibly to attacks. These same features can make democracies attractive terrorist targets.
    
    \item Authoritarian regimes may be better able to repress some forms of terrorism but can be more vulnerable to other strategies, especially intimidation in weak or contested territories.
\end{enumerate}

The authors distinguish their strategic level of analysis from both tactical and structural explanations. Tactical measures such as intelligence collection and target protection may be useful regardless of the terrorists' strategy. Structural conditions such as poverty, education, political exclusion, international conflict, or ideological indoctrination may help explain why terrorist organizations arise. The article's distinctive contribution lies between these levels: it explains why existing organizations select particular forms of violence and how those forms of violence are intended to produce political effects.

\subsection{Overall conclusion}

Kydd and Walter's main contribution is to show that terrorism is not a single strategy. The same violent act can serve very different purposes depending on the audience and the uncertainty terrorists seek to manipulate. Effective counterterrorism therefore cannot rely on a universal formula.

The five strategies can be summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Attrition}: convince the enemy that continued resistance will be too costly. The best responses are selective concessions where appropriate, targeted retaliation, target hardening, weapons denial, and psychological resilience.
    \item \textbf{Intimidation}: convince civilians that terrorists can punish disobedience and the government cannot protect them. The best responses are territorial control, protection of vulnerable actors, and stronger law enforcement.
    \item \textbf{Provocation}: induce indiscriminate government violence to radicalize the population. The best response is precise, intelligence-driven counterterrorism that minimizes collateral damage.
    \item \textbf{Spoiling}: undermine peace by creating doubt about moderates' willingness or ability to enforce an agreement. The best responses are trust-building, monitoring, enforcement, and institutional solutions to commitment problems.
    \item \textbf{Outbidding}: demonstrate greater commitment than rival groups to win domestic support. The best responses are reducing factional competition and making peaceful political strategies more rewarding.
\end{itemize}

The overarching policy lesson is that governments must understand the political objective and signaling logic behind terrorist violence before reacting. Terrorists often succeed precisely because their targets respond predictably. A poorly calibrated counterterrorist response can become part of the terrorist strategy itself. The most effective policies are therefore those that deny terrorists the informational and political effects they seek while improving intelligence, protecting civilians, and avoiding actions that create new support for extremist organizations.
